From Idea to MVP: Building a Prototype That Proves Your Value

23 September 2026 · Dagitab: InnovFest 2026, CORE NCR Hackathon · CORE Talent Accelerator · 2.5 hours

Every team ran a compressed design sprint on its own product in one afternoon: understand and map, sketch, decide and storyboard, prototype, test, then iterate. We separated a prototype from an MVP, drew the line between must-haves and everything else, and planned a demo that proves one complete flow instead of promising fifteen features. This page has the templates and checklists to finish the job before demo day.

If you forget everything else

A prototype tests your design. An MVP tests your demand.
Build the lowest fidelity that answers your current question.
One complete flow beats five half flows.

The winning team is rarely the one that built the most. It is the one that proved the most with the least.

Two words that decide what you build

Prototype

A visual or interactive model of your idea, built to test your design and user flow. Like a movie set: it looks real from the front, and that is the point. A paper sketch already counts.

  • Explore: try several options cheaply.
  • Communicate: get the whole team seeing the same thing.
  • Test: put it in front of a real person and watch.

MVP (Minimum Viable Product)

A functional version of your product, released to real users to test demand.

  • Minimum: as small as possible.
  • Viable: it actually works.
  • Product: someone can actually use it.

Key differences

PrototypeMVP
PurposeValidates design, usability and the conceptValidates real user adoption
FunctionalityA mock-up or simulation, no real backendA working product with the essential core features
AudienceYour team, mentors, judgesReal early users
Time and costDays, almost freeWeeks or months of real engineering
An MVP is not half a product. Half a bridge helps no one cross; a small, complete footbridge does. If the goal is transportation, the first version is a skateboard you can ride today, not one wheel of a car. Small, but complete end to end.

For a hackathon, a clickable prototype or a working demo of one flow is a winning outcome. A true MVP usually comes after, and that is normal.

The design sprint, compressed

The design sprint was created by Jake Knapp at GV (Google Ventures) to answer big product questions fast. It normally takes five days, one step per day. A deadline plus a fixed order kills endless debate: you build, you test, and the user decides. We ran the same steps, in the same order, in one afternoon.

  1. Understand and map: understand the problem, map the user's journey.
  2. Sketch and ideate: ask How Might We questions, draw solutions on paper.
  3. Decide and storyboard: choose what to build, put it in order.
  4. Prototype: build a realistic fake of the core flow.
  5. Test: watch real people try it.

Then iterate: turn what you saw into one change at a time. Throughout the session the worked example was CanteenGo, a pre-order app for a school canteen. Run each step on your own product.

1. Understand and map

Understand the problem

Challenge

What is broken today, and for whom?

Goal

What does success look like, in one measurable line?

Sprint questions

What must be true for this to work? A prototype exists to answer these.

Pick your primary persona: the one user you design for first. Write down what they want to get done and what gets in their way.

Write the core value in one sentence

This product helps [WHO] to [DO ONE THING] so that [BENEFIT].

Example: CanteenGo helps students get their canteen food with less than five minutes of waiting.

If your team cannot fill this in one sentence, that is the first problem to fix, and fixing it costs paper, not code.

Map the core user journey

The shortest path from opening the product to getting the value: three to six boxes in a straight line.

[ Browse menu ] -> [ Pre-order ] -> [ Ready notification ] -> [ Pick up ]

That line is your core user experience. If only one thing works on demo day, it must be this line.

2. Sketch and ideate

How Might We questions

Reframe the challenge as open questions, then give each answer a short name.

  • How might we let users finish the core task in seconds?
  • How might we tell users something is ready without making them wait and watch?
  • How might we help the people on the other side without slowing down the old way?

Wireframes, the folded-paper way

A wireframe is a black-and-white skeleton of a screen: boxes and labels, no colors, no fonts, no design talent required.

  1. Fold one sheet of paper into panels, one panel per journey box.
  2. Sketch each step as one rough screen. Minutes, not hours.
  3. Weave your best How Might We answers into the screens.
  4. Compare sketches as a team and discuss the idea, not the drawing.
Ugly is correct. Ugly means fast, fast means disposable, and disposable means honest feedback. Sketching also has no language barrier: everyone on the team can contribute equally.

3. Decide and storyboard: scope the MVP

The step that decides whether you demo something working or apologize on stage. Scope is everything you decided to build for this version, and nothing more. At a hackathon, time and budget are fixed, so scope is the only lever you can move.

The disappearing test

  1. List every feature you have imagined. Every single one.
  2. For each, ask: if this feature disappeared, does the core value still work? Yes means nice-to-have. No means must-have.
  3. Draw the MVP line. Must-haves that serve the core journey go above it. Everything else goes below, into a Later list. Parked, not deleted.
  4. Put the keepers in journey order. That ordered row is your storyboard: for each frame, write the user action and the system response.
  5. Pick your demo-day format and write the question it will answer.
Watch for trap cards. A feature that sounds essential but is a large integration, such as real online payments, can eat your whole hackathon. If paying cash at pickup still proves the core value, payments go on the Later list.

Common hackathon cuts

  • Login and accounts (fake it)
  • Settings
  • Onboarding tutorials
  • Admin panels
  • Push notifications
  • Multiple user types (pick one)
  • Edge cases
  • Dark mode

MoSCoW (Must, Should, Could, Won't) is the formal version. For a hackathon, the honest version is Must versus Later.

The fidelity ladder

Fidelity is how finished a prototype looks and behaves. Choose by the question you need answered.

FidelityFormatThe question it answersEffort
LowPaper wireframeDoes the flow make sense? Do we agree?Minutes
MediumMock-up (static, real colors and layout) or clickable prototype (linked screens, no real backend)Can a user get through without help? Does it look real?Hours, no code
HighFunctional demo (a real working slice with real logic or data)Does the risky logic actually work?Days, so build only the one risky slice
The rule: build the lowest fidelity that answers your current question. Never code what a clickable prototype can answer.

Tools, free or free-tier

Wireframes

Pen and paper (the best tool you have), a whiteboard, Excalidraw, Balsamiq, Figma wireframe kits.

Mock-ups

Figma, Canva.

Clickable prototypes

Figma prototype mode: link your frames and present on a phone.

Functional demos

No-code builders (Glide, Softr, Bubble, AppSheet), AI-assisted coding tools (v0, Lovable, Bolt, Replit, Claude, Cursor), or your own stack. Describe the one core flow and build only that.

Tools change every year. The ladder and the rule do not.

4. Prototype: one complete thread

Build the core flow only

Front to back, even if ugly. One complete thread beats five half threads. Judges do not count features; they look for one that works end to end.

Timebox everything

Every task gets a deadline in minutes, not "until it is done." For example: 40 minutes for the menu screen, then move on.

Fake what you can

Hard-code the data, use placeholder content, make "Login" a button that just opens the app. Build the front of the movie set, not the house.

Divide the team

Not everyone codes. One person builds, one prepares the user test, one drafts the pitch and the demo run. Parallel work beats five people watching one laptop.

Build plan template

  • The screens, in journey order
  • Who builds what
  • What gets faked, written down explicitly
  • A timebox per task, in minutes
  • Who prepares the user test, and who drafts the pitch

5. Test: the 5-minute user test

No lab, no forms, no budget. One stranger and five minutes. Run it as soon as your prototype is ready, and before your pitch is final.

  1. Find one person who is not on your team.
  2. Give them one task, in one sentence. For example: "Order food for your break."
  3. Stay quiet and watch. Do not help, do not explain, do not defend. Silence is data.
  4. Note where they get stuck. Hesitations, wrong taps, stuck points, the face they make. Watch what they do, not what they say.
  5. Ask open questions afterward. "What did you expect to happen here?" Never "Did you like it?", because people are polite.
  6. Pick one fix. Make it, then test again.

FieldExample
WhoThree people from another team

Iterate: one fix at a time

  • Feedback is a to-do list, not a verdict. One confusing step means fix that step, not scrap the product.
  • Separate signal from noise. Every tester stuck on the same step is signal; one person's color preference is noise.
  • Change one thing, then test again. Change ten things at once and you learn nothing.
  • Build, measure, learn. This loop from the Lean Startup is how serious products are made. The MVP is just the vehicle.

If the core value itself gets shrugs, you just saved months. Rework the value sentence and keep the skills.

Demo-day preparation

The demo-day format was five minutes of pitch, one minute of demo, and five minutes of questions. Your prototype is the proof inside the pitch.

The pitch: four beats

  1. The problem, in one line
  2. The core flow, live
  3. What a real user told you
  4. What you build next

Lead with the value, not the tech.

How the judging criteria map to your sprint

CriterionWeightYour evidence
Relevance15%Challenge, goal, target users
Innovation and creativity25%Your How Might We sketches
Feasibility and practicality25%The MVP line and storyboard: realistic scope, a logical path
Viability and sustainability20%The Later list as a growth path, plus your user tests
Presentation and communication15%The pitch and the live demo

No criterion counts features.

Checklist

  • Core value sentence, final
  • Problem line for the pitch, written
  • Core flow works end to end, in your chosen format
  • Demo rehearsed as one live pass through the core flow, inside one minute
  • At least one user test run, and one fix made from it
  • One quote or observation from a real user ready for the pitch
  • Later list ready as your answer to "what is next?"
  • MVP line ready as your answer to "why not feature X?"
  • Prototype loaded on a phone and on a laptop
  • Team agreed on who presents, who drives the demo, who takes questions
Never apologize on stage. Not "Sorry, this part is not working yet," but "We focused on proving the core value first." Same fact; one sounds like failure, the other sounds like discipline.

The one-flow commitment

Write the one core flow your team will have working by demo day, plus the format. For example: "Order flow, browse to notification, clickable prototype in Figma." Everyone signs it. Pin a photo in your team chat, and look at it whenever someone says "let us add one more feature."

After the hackathon

  1. Keep testing: five minutes, one stranger, one task, every time something changes.
  2. Move up the fidelity ladder only when a new question needs it.
  3. Pull the next item off the Later list only after the core flow is proven.
  4. When the prototype has answered its questions, release a small, complete MVP to real users and test demand.
